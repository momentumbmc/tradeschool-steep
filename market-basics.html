5-minute plain-English starter guide · Zero prior background needed ↓

New to the stock market? Start right here, in plain English.

If terms like auction, order flow, or institutional inventory already make sense to you, you can skip straight to our flagship syllabus. If none of that language feels familiar yet, welcome. This page exists for one job: giving someone with zero market background an honest, jargon-free starting point, not a sales pitch.

01 · The starting idea

What is a financial market, really?

A market, in everyday life, is a place where people buy and sell things. A vegetable market trades vegetables. A financial market trades financial assets instead: small pieces of ownership in companies, loans, and currencies.

It exists for a simple reason. Companies and governments need capital to grow, build, and operate. Everyday people and institutions with spare savings want a way to put money to work instead of letting it sit idle. A financial market is where those two needs meet and agree on a price.

02 · Ownership & live auction

What is the stock market, specifically?

A stock, also called a share, is a small ownership slice of a business. If a company has one crore shares outstanding and you own one share, you own one ten-millionth of that company. Companies sell shares first when they go public to raise funds, and after that, existing shares trade between buyers and sellers on exchanges such as the NSE or BSE.

Nobody sets the share price by decree. It moves because, at every moment, buyers and sellers slightly disagree on what a fair price is, and each trade happens when two people agree. Multiply that negotiation across millions of trades a day and you get a moving price. Underneath everything, a stock market is a live auction running continuously. That single idea is the exact foundation TradeSchool's entire curriculum is built on.

03 · Two distinct skills

Investing versus trading, in one line

Investing means holding ownership in a company for years, relying on the business growing steadily over time. Trading means buying and selling over much shorter windows, such as days, hours, or minutes, based on how price is moving right now rather than the multi-year health of the business. Neither is more legitimate than the other. They are distinct skills, and mixing them up is one of the commonest ways beginners lose money without noticing which game they entered.

04 · Informed decisions vs. guessing

Why understanding this matters first

Anyone can download an app and place a trade tomorrow without reading a page of education. Most people do exactly that. It is a major reason why SEBI's public studies found that 7 out of 10 intraday equity traders and over 9 out of 10 individual derivatives traders lost money. Learning how markets work does not guarantee profits, nothing does, yet entering without understanding is closer to guessing than informed decision making.
